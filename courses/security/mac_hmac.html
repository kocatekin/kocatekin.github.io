The magic stamp

Barney writes a letter to Harry. He owns a magic stamp, and he is the only person in the world who has it.

Imagine you have a magic stamp that only you own. When you press it on a letter, it leaves a wax seal that is made from the words of that exact letter.

  • 1Only you can make the seal, because only you have the stamp. That's your private key.
  • 2Everyone can check the seal, because a picture of your stamp hangs on the school wall. That's your public key.
  • 3If someone changes even one word, the seal no longer matches the letter. Your friend sees it right away.
  • 4You can't later say "I never sent that". The seal is yours and nobody else could have made it.

1 · The problem

Why we need digital signatures

On the internet, anyone can type anything. An email can say it's from your bank. A file can say it's an update from Microsoft. A message can say "Your grade is 95" when the teacher wrote 65. We need a way to answer two questions about any message:

Who really sent this? Has anyone changed it since?

A digital signature answers both. When the signature checks out, you get three guarantees:

AuthenticityIt really came from the person who claims to have sent it.
IntegrityNot a single letter has changed since it was signed.
Non-repudiationThe sender can't later say "That wasn't me."

How is this different from signing with a pen?

Handwritten signatureDigital signature
Looks the same on every document?Yes, the same squiggle every timeNo, it's different for every message
Can someone copy it onto another document?Yes, with some practiceNo, a copied signature won't match the new message
Shows if the document was changed later?NoYes, any change breaks it
Who checks it?A person looking at it, maybe an expertAny computer, in a split second
The one sentence to remember

A digital signature is tied to both the person (their private key) and the exact message. Change either one and it stops working.

2 · Quick recap

Two keys, two jobs

You've already met key pairs in public-key cryptography. Signatures use the same pair. For signatures, think of the jobs like this:

Private keyMakes signaturesKept secret by its owner, never shared. Like the magic stamp, or your own pen that nobody else can hold.
Public keyChecks signaturesGiven to everyone. Like the picture of the stamp on the wall. It can check a seal but can't make one.
Don't mix it up with encryption

With encryption, the public key locks and the private key unlocks, so only the owner can read. With signatures, it's the other way around: the private key signs and the public key checks, so everyone can verify that only the owner signed.

Why sign the fingerprint instead of the whole message?

Remember from the hashing lecture: a fingerprint is short, always the same size, and changes completely if the message changes. Signing a small fingerprint is much faster than signing a whole video or a 500-page PDF, and it's just as safe.

Notice that the signature is not secret. The message and its signature can travel over the open internet. The signature isn't there to hide anything. It's there to prove who sent the message and that it arrived unchanged.

5 · The trust problem

Whose key is it?

There's one trick left for a cheater. What if the attacker makes their own key pair and tells the office: "Hi, I'm Barney, here is my new public key"? Then the attacker's signatures would check out as "Barney's".

So we need a trusted party that vouches: "This public key really belongs to Barney." That trusted party is a certificate authority (CA), and the document it gives out is a certificate.

In real lifeYour ID card is issued by the government. People trust your photo and name because they trust the government that signed off on them.
OnlineA certificate is a digital ID card: a name plus a public key, signed by a CA that everyone's computer already trusts.

CAs are arranged like a family tree. A few root CAs come pre-installed in your phone and browser. They vouch for intermediate CAs, which in turn vouch for websites. Your browser follows that chain upward until it reaches a root it already trusts.

7 · Weak spots

What can go wrong

Digital signatures are very hard to forge. When things go wrong, it's almost always because of something around them:

  • The private key gets stolen. Then the thief can sign as you. This is why important keys live on smart cards or special hardware, like the card used for e-İmza.
  • A fake public key is trusted. Without a proper certificate, a cheater can pretend their key is yours (section 5).
  • Nobody actually checks. A signature only helps if the software verifies it. Bugs that skip the check have happened in real products.
  • Only part of the message is signed. If the amount or date isn't covered by the signature, it can be changed.
  • Old, broken methods. Fingerprint methods that are now broken, like MD5 and SHA-1, shouldn't be used for signatures anymore.
Looking ahead

Future quantum computers could break today's signature methods. New "post-quantum" signature methods have already been standardized and are slowly being rolled out. The big picture stays the same: private key signs, public key checks.

9 · Glossary

Key terms

Digital signatureA piece of data that proves who sent a message and that it wasn't changed.
Private keyThe secret key that makes signatures. Only the owner has it.
Public keyThe shared key that checks signatures. Anyone can have it.
Fingerprint (hash)A short summary of a message. This is what actually gets signed.
VerificationChecking that a signature matches the message and the sender's public key.
Non-repudiationThe sender can't deny having signed.
CertificateA digital ID card: a name plus a public key, vouched for by a CA.
Certificate authority (CA)A trusted organization that issues certificates.
Chain of trustWebsite → intermediate CA → root CA your device already trusts.
