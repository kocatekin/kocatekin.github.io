Stoic Content Calendar

30 Days of Stoicism

A balanced month of practical guidance, core philosophy, memorable stories, reflective exercises, and carefully selected quotes. Each day includes a distinct angle and a ready-to-use content prompt.

01
PracticalCarousel

Control the Controllable

Introduce the Stoic distinction between what belongs to us and what does not.

Content promptList five frustrations from an ordinary day, then separate them into “within my control” and “outside my control.” End with one concrete action.

02
PhilosophyShort essay

Virtue Is the Goal

Explain why Stoicism is not primarily about feeling calm, but about living wisely and ethically.

Content promptContrast “I want peace” with “I want to respond well.” Show how wisdom, courage, justice, and self-control shape decisions.

03
StoryNarrative post

Epictetus and the Lamp

Tell the story of Epictetus replacing a stolen iron lamp with a cheaper clay one.

Content promptUse the story to ask: how much suffering comes from expecting possessions to remain permanently ours?

04
ExerciseJournal prompt

The Evening Review

Adapt Seneca’s habit of reviewing his conduct at the end of each day.

Content promptAsk three questions: What did I do well? Where did I lose control? What will I do differently tomorrow?

05
QuoteQuote card + caption

“You Have Power Over Your Mind”

Use Marcus Aurelius to distinguish inner judgment from external circumstance.

Content promptExplain that power over the mind is not emotional suppression; it is the ability to question the story we attach to events.

06
PracticalChecklist

Pause Before Replying

Turn Stoic restraint into a simple communication habit.

Content promptTeach a three-step pause: notice the reaction, name the impression, choose the response. Apply it to messages, meetings, and arguments.

07
PhilosophyExplainer

What Stoics Mean by “Impressions”

Explain that initial impressions arise automatically, but assent is a second step.

Content promptUse an example: “They ignored my message” becomes “They do not respect me.” Show where interpretation enters.

08
StoryReel script

Marcus Aurelius in the Plague

Describe the emperor governing amid war, disease, and political pressure.

Content promptFocus on duty under pressure: leadership is not waiting for ideal conditions, but acting responsibly in imperfect ones.

09
Exercise24-hour challenge

A Day Without Complaining

Invite followers to observe complaints without turning the exercise into forced positivity.

Content promptEach time you complain, restate the issue as a fact and add one useful response: “This happened; here is what I can do.”

10
QuoteMinimal graphic

“We Suffer More in Imagination”

Use Seneca’s line to explore anticipatory anxiety and mental rehearsal.

Content promptSeparate preparation from rumination. Preparation produces a plan; rumination repeats a fear without improving action.

11
PracticalHow-to post

Turn Delay Into Training

Use queues, traffic, and waiting as opportunities to practise patience.

Content promptGive three alternatives to irritation: observe breathing, review priorities, or practise attention without reaching for the phone.

12
PhilosophyMyth-busting

Stoicism Is Not Emotional Numbness

Clarify the difference between emotion, judgment, and destructive passion.

Content promptExplain that Stoics did not aim to become cold; they aimed to avoid being ruled by distorted judgments.

13
StoryHistorical thread

Cato and the Cost of Principle

Present Cato as an example of consistency, discipline, and uncompromising public ethics.

Content promptAsk where firmness becomes integrity and where it becomes rigidity. Keep the story morally complex.

14
ExerciseReflection worksheet

Negative Visualization

Practise briefly imagining the absence of something valued to deepen gratitude.

Content promptChoose one ordinary good—health, a person, a home, a routine. Imagine its absence for two minutes, then return attention to its present value.

15
QuoteMid-month post

“No Person Is Free Who Is Not Master of Himself”

Use Epictetus to connect freedom with discipline rather than unrestricted choice.

Content promptDiscuss one modern dependency—notifications, approval, anger, consumption—and ask what mastery would look like.

16
PracticalDecision framework

Use the Four Virtues as a Filter

Convert Stoic ethics into a practical decision-making tool.

Content promptBefore a difficult choice, ask: Is it wise? Is it just? Does it require courage? Does it show self-control?

17
PhilosophyConcept post

Amor Fati Without Passivity

Explain loving fate as active acceptance of reality, not approval of every event.

Content promptShow the sequence: acknowledge what happened, refuse useless denial, then decide the most honorable next move.

18
StoryCharacter study

Musonius Rufus and Simple Living

Introduce the teacher who linked philosophy to food, clothing, work, and daily discipline.

Content promptShow how philosophy becomes credible only when visible in ordinary habits, not just elegant language.

19
ExerciseBehavior experiment

Voluntary Discomfort

Use mild, safe discomfort to weaken dependency on comfort.

Content promptChoose one modest exercise: a simple meal, a cold finish to a shower, walking instead of driving, or leaving the phone behind.

20
QuoteQuote analysis

“The Obstacle Becomes the Way”

Explore Marcus Aurelius’s idea that impediments can become material for virtue.

Content promptGive examples: criticism becomes restraint, delay becomes patience, failure becomes clearer judgment.

21
PracticalWorkplace guide

Handle Difficult People Stoically

Apply Marcus Aurelius’s morning preparation to conflict and cooperation.

Content promptPrepare for friction without contempt. Define your boundary, your duty, and the tone you want to preserve.

22
PhilosophyDeep dive

The Stoic View of Time

Explore the difference between possessing time and merely spending it unconsciously.

Content promptUse Seneca’s argument: life is not necessarily short, but much of it is lost to distraction, delay, and borrowed priorities.

23
StoryPersonal-style narrative

The Archer and the Wind

Use the Stoic archer analogy: we control preparation and aim, not the final flight of the arrow.

Content promptApply it to publishing, interviews, exams, relationships, or business outcomes.

24
ExerciseMorning routine

Premeditatio Malorum

Rehearse likely setbacks so they feel less shocking and more manageable.

Content promptName one challenge likely to arise today, then write your preferred response before it happens.

25
QuoteReflection card

“Waste No More Time Arguing What a Good Person Should Be”

Use Marcus Aurelius to shift from identity claims to observable conduct.

Content promptAsk followers to choose one virtue and demonstrate it through one visible act today.

26
PracticalDigital habit

Practise Digital Temperance

Apply Stoic self-command to feeds, notifications, and compulsive checking.

Content promptCreate a simple rule: no phone for the first 30 minutes, notifications off, and deliberate check-in windows.

27
PhilosophyEthics post

Justice Is Central to Stoicism

Correct the self-help distortion that reduces Stoicism to personal resilience.

Content promptExplain that a Stoic life includes fairness, service, cooperation, and responsibility toward others.

28
StoryTransformation story

Zeno’s Shipwreck

Tell how commercial loss helped redirect Zeno toward philosophy.

Content promptAvoid romanticizing loss. Focus instead on how an unwanted event can reveal a different path.

29
ExerciseMonthly review

Your Stoic Scorecard

Review the month through conduct rather than mood.

Content promptRate yourself from 1–5 on wisdom, courage, justice, and self-control. Add one example and one improvement for each.

30
QuoteClosing post

“Begin at Once to Live”

Close the month with Seneca’s reminder that philosophy must become practice now.

Content promptInvite followers to choose one habit from the month and continue it for the next 30 days.

Suggested publishing rhythm: Use carousel posts for explanations, reels for stories, static cards for quotes, and stories or downloadable worksheets for exercises. Keep captions practical by ending each post with one question, one action, or one reflection prompt.
